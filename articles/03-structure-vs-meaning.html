From data to meaning · the dividing line

Structure the machine can infer — meaning only a person can decide.

Point an agent at the warehouse and a surprising amount of the structure drafts itself. But the handful of decisions that make an answer correct are exactly the ones the data can't settle. That handful is the whole game — and it's where a person is not optional.

The machine can infer from the datadrafts itself

  • The tables and their columns.NetPrice, UnitPrice, Quantity look like amounts; ExchangeRate like a factor.
  • Cardinality.CurrencyCode has 5 values — a closed set; ProductKey has thousands — an identity key.
  • Keys and joins.sales.ProductKey → product — the relationships between things.
  • The odd values."Online" sitting among the countries; sales = orders × orderrows.

Only a person can decide the meaningyou author

  • "Revenue" means net, not gross.Both columns exist; nothing in the data says which one you report.
  • Convert before summing.Five currencies; a raw sum is meaningless — but the data won't tell you that.
  • "Online" is a channel, not a market.Segregate and disclose it — or silently mislead a by-country total.
  • Never add the two shapes together.The duplicate facts are a trap only a rule can close.

The machine proposes the structure; a person authors the meaning. The agent drafts much of the shape; you write the few rules that decide whether the number is right — and those are exactly the ones a naive text-to-SQL bot gets silently wrong.
